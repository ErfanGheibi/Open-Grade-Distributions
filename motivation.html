Motivation

Pushing for Transparency

An expensive thing to buy blind

A university degree is one of the largest purchases most people ever make, and it keeps getting more expensive. Tuition and fees. Rent near campus. Years of forgone income. And underneath all of that, a cost that doesn't show up on any invoice: time, often spent during some of the most formative and opportune years of your life. You commit to all of it up front, on very little information, and you don't find out what you actually got until you're most of the way through paying for it.

Unit guides and course handbooks aren't much help here. They're written by the institution selling you the course, so naturally they describe it in the best possible light. Word-of-mouth and online reviews fill some of the gap, but they're subjective, thin in number, and not always reliable — one glowing review from a friend or one bitter rant from someone who failed doesn't tell you much about what actually happens to the hundreds of students who take a course each term.

Don't students deserve to know that a particular course has had a 30% fail rate two years running? That the "core" subject everyone dreads is actually fine? That the highly-rated elective everyone recommends quietly destroys people's WAM? That information already exists, sitting in a university's own administrative records. It's just never been the university's job, or in most cases its interest, to hand it to you before you enrol.

Learning from other universities

This isn't a radical idea, and it isn't untested. A number of universities already publish some form of grade data publicly, rather than keeping it locked inside internal systems only staff can see, and some of it has come from students who had to build the tools themselves after asking. A few examples:

  • University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
  • UC Berkeley
  • UCLA
  • UC Irvine
  • University of Mary Washington
  • Purdue University
  • Michigan State University
  • Virginia Tech
  • Georgia Institute of Technology

And grades are only one piece of what could reasonably be public. Tutor-to-student ratios. How long classes actually run. Even a small, honest glimpse of the course material itself. MIT's OpenCourseWare is the boldest example of that last idea: since 2001, it has published the core teaching material for virtually its entire curriculum online, free, for anyone, whether or not they were ever admitted. If the most prestigious university on the planet is willing to publish such highly sought-after data, it's hard to argue a public university publishing its own grade data is asking too much.

This project is a small piece of that same idea, scoped to the one thing I could actually get my hands on: what grade you're likely to get, based on what happened to everyone who took the course before you. It won't tell you whether a course is right for you. But it's one honest source of information in a decision that, for most students, only gets made once — and I'd rather add that than add nothing.
